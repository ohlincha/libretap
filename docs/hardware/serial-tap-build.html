Serial tap: build guide

A passive DB-9 tap for LibreTap: the laptop listens to both directions of an RS-232 link and cannot send anything.

How it works

RS-232 uses one wire per direction: DB-9 pin 3 carries what the PC sends, pin 2 what it receives; pin 5 is ground. The tap is a short extension cable (all nine pins straight through) with two extra leads. Lead X connects pin 3 to the receive pin (2) of adapter X; lead Y connects pin 2 to the receive pin of adapter Y. Nothing is connected to the adapters' transmit pin (3), so the laptop cannot put anything on the line.

Time: about 2 hours the first time. Not yet tested on a real instrument.

Tools: soldering iron with stand, solder (0.5–1 mm, rosin core), wire stripper or sharp knife, side cutters, a helping hand or a vice to hold the connector, multimeter with continuity (beep) mode, lighter or hot-air gun for the heat-shrink (or the side of the iron), masking tape and a pen, cable ties.

Ribbon colours

Rainbow ribbon follows the resistor colour code. Use core n for pin n: then the colour tells the pin. Check the order on your ribbon before starting; tear off the black (10th) core.

Separate cores by nicking the ribbon between two cores with a knife and pulling them apart by hand.

Soldering in brief

  • Safety. Work in a ventilated room or with fume extraction; do not breathe the smoke. Wear eye protection (clipped wire ends fly). The iron goes back into its stand every time you put it down; the tip is above 300 °C. Leaded solder: wash your hands afterwards and do not eat at the bench.
  • Solder. 60/40 tin–lead melts lower and is easier for beginners; lead-free is required in some workshops. Either works.
  • Temperature. 330–350 °C for leaded solder, 350–380 °C for lead-free.
  • Tin the tip. Wipe the hot tip on the damp sponge or brass wool, then melt a little solder onto it. A shiny tip transfers heat; a dull, black one does not.
  • Tin the wire. Strip 3 mm, twist the strands, heat them and feed in a little solder until the strands are coated. Tinned ends go into cups without loose strands.
  • Heat the joint, not the solder. Hold the tip against cup and wire, then feed solder into the joint (not onto the tip). 2–3 seconds is enough. Remove the solder, then the iron.
  • Don't move it until the solder has set (a second or two). A joint moved while setting looks grainy and is weak.
  • Heat-shrink goes on before soldering. Slide a piece onto the wire, keep it away from the heat while soldering, then slide it over the cup and shrink it.
Practice first. Solder two scrap wires together and one wire into a spare cup before starting. Pull on them: a good joint holds.

1. Find the pins

You solder on the back of each connector (the solder side). Seen from there, the pin numbers are mirrored compared with the front, and male and female connectors are mirrored to each other. Most connectors have the numbers moulded into the plastic next to the cups: trust those numbers over any drawing.

  1. Hold each connector with the solder side towards you and the wider row of cups at the top.
  2. Find pin 1 on each and compare with Figure 3. Mark pin 1 with a pen dot on the plastic.
Check. Female, solder side: pin 1 top left. Male, solder side: pin 1 top right.

2. Prepare the ribbon

  1. Cut 15 cm of ribbon and tear off the black core: 9 cores remain (brown … white). This is the pass-through.
  2. Cut two 50 cm pieces for the leads and tear off two 2-core strips: orange + yellow (lead X) and red + brown (lead Y). Label them X and Y with tape at both ends.
  3. Split each end of all cores 3 cm apart, strip 3 mm and tin each end.

3. Solder the pass-through

  1. Clamp the female connector solder side up. Tin all nine cups (Figure 6, step 1).
  2. Slide an 8 mm piece of heat-shrink onto each of the nine cores.
  3. Solder brown into cup 1, red into cup 2 … white into cup 9. Start with the bottom row so the iron does not have to reach past finished joints.
  4. Do the same on the male connector at the other end of the ribbon: brown into 1 … white into 9. Do not twist the ribbon so far that cores cross.
  5. Do not shrink the sleeves yet on cups 2, 3 and 5 of the female end: they get more wires in step 4. Shrink the others.
Check. Each colour sits in the cup with its number on both connectors (Figure 3). No solder bridges between neighbouring cups. Sleeves shrunk on 1, 4, 6, 7, 8, 9 (female) and on all nine (male).

4. Solder the leads X and Y

  1. On the tap's female end, reheat cup 3 and add the orange core of lead X.
  2. Reheat cup 2 and add the red core of lead Y.
  3. Twist the yellow (X) and brown (Y) cores together, tin them, and add them to cup 5. Cup 5 now holds three wires. If the heat-shrink no longer fits over cup 5, leave it off there and make sure no strand touches cup 4, 6 or 9.
  4. At the other end of lead X: orange into cup 2 of plug X, yellow into cup 5.
  5. At the other end of lead Y: red into cup 2 of plug Y, brown into cup 5.
  6. Leave all other cups of X and Y empty. Pin 3 on X and Y must stay empty: it is the adapter's transmit pin.
  7. Shrink the remaining sleeves.
Check. Lead X: orange from tap 3 to X 2, yellow from tap 5 to X 5. Lead Y: red from tap 2 to Y 2, brown from tap 5 to Y 5. X and Y have only cups 2 and 5 used.

5. Test with the multimeter

Set the multimeter to continuity (the beep symbol •))). Touch the two probes together: it beeps. On the male end the pins stick out; on female ends, push a thin wire or a paper clip into the socket and touch that.

6. Close the hoods

  1. Lay the cable into the hood's strain relief and screw the two halves together (or slide a one-piece hood forward).
  2. The tap's female end has three cables in one hood (ribbon, X, Y). If they do not fit, close the hood around the ribbon and fix X and Y to it with a cable tie just behind the hood, so no pull reaches the cups.
  3. Fit the thumb screws (or the hex standoffs supplied with the hood).
Check. Repeat the first and the last three rows of the table: closing a hood can pull a wire loose.

7. Use it at the instrument

  1. Close the vendor software (OPUS, …).
  2. Unplug the instrument cable from the PC's COM port. Plug the tap's female end onto the COM port and the instrument cable into the tap's male end.
  3. Plug X and Y onto the two USB–RS-232 adapters, and the adapters into the laptop. Run the laptop on battery (avoids a ground loop between the laptop's and the PC's mains supplies).
  4. Start LibreTap. Connections shows Serial tap ready and names the two adapters. If the PC and instrument sides are the wrong way round, press Swap sides.
  5. Start the vendor software and check that it talks to the instrument as before.
  6. Unsure of the speed: press Find speed while the software talks to the instrument.
