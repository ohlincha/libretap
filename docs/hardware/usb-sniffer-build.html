USB sniffer: build guide

A Raspberry Pi Pico spliced into a USB extension cable. It records the USB traffic between the instrument PC and the instrument for LibreTap.

Limits

  • Low speed (1.5 Mbit/s) and full speed (12 Mbit/s) only. Most instrument USB links are full speed. A high-speed link (480 Mbit/s) cannot be recorded with this sniffer. On Linux, lsusb -t shows the speed (12M or 1.5M is fine, 480M is not).
  • If the instrument's USB device is a USB–serial chip (FTDI, PL2303, CP210x, CH340), the serial tap on its serial side is the simpler option.

Time: about 1 hour. Not yet tested on a real instrument.

Tools: soldering iron with stand, thin solder (0.5–0.8 mm), sharp craft knife, side cutters, wire stripper, multimeter with continuity (beep) mode, something to hold the work (helping hand, tape on the bench).

The instrument normally plugs into the PC with an A plug. The extension sits between them: its A plug into the PC, the instrument's cable into its A socket. If the instrument has a fixed cable with a different plug, use a sacrificial cable of that type instead and splice it the same way.

Firmware

The Pico needs LibreTap's firmware, USB Sniffer Stream. Flashing takes a minute and needs no tools: see the separate guide Pico firmware: flashing. Flash before or after building; the wiring does not change it.

Soldering in brief

  • Safety. Work in a ventilated room or with fume extraction. Wear eye protection (clipped wire ends fly). The iron goes back into its stand every time you put it down. Leaded solder: wash your hands afterwards.
  • Solder. 60/40 tin–lead is easier for beginners; lead-free works too.
  • Temperature. 330–350 °C for leaded solder, 350–380 °C for lead-free.
  • Tin the tip: wipe it on the damp sponge or brass wool, melt a little solder onto it.
  • Tin the wire before joining: strip, twist the strands, coat them with solder.
  • Heat the joint, not the solder. Tip against pad and wire, feed solder into the joint, 2–3 s, remove solder, then iron.
  • Don't move it until the solder has set.
  • Insulate every joint. Heat-shrink must be slid on before soldering; on the splice it cannot go over the uncut wires, so use tape there.
Practice first. Solder two scrap wires together and one wire into a pad of a scrap board, if you have one. Pull on them: a good joint holds.

1. Identify the wires

Inside a USB 2.0 cable there are four wires. The colours below are the convention, not a guarantee: confirm with the multimeter before soldering.

You confirm the colours in section 2, once the wires are exposed: multimeter in continuity mode, one probe on a contact of the extension's A plug, the other on a bare wire.

Expected. Contact 1 ↔ red, 2 ↔ white, 3 ↔ green, 4 ↔ black. If your cable differs, use the measured colours from then on: what matters is D+ (contact 3) → Pico pin 14, D- (contact 2) → pin 15, GND (contact 4) → pin 13.

2. Open the cable

  1. Choose a point about 30 cm from the A socket end. Score the outer jacket around the cable twice, 3 cm apart, and once lengthwise between the two. Do not press hard: the wires lie just underneath.
  2. Peel the jacket off. Fold the foil and the braid (if any) back; do not cut them off.
  3. Spread the four wires. On green, white and black, cut two shallow rings 3–4 mm apart and peel the insulation off between them. Stagger the three windows so the joints are not side by side.
  4. Leave red closed.
  5. Confirm the colours (section 1): probe each contact of the A plug against the windows.
Check. Each window shows bright copper; no strands are cut. Continuity from the A plug through to the A socket is still there for all four contacts (contact n of the plug to contact n of the socket).

3. Solder the wires to the cable

  1. Cut three wires, 10 cm each (green, white, black if you have them; otherwise label them).
  2. Strip 1 cm at one end, tin it, and wrap it once around the matching copper window: green on green, white on white, black on black.
  3. Solder each wrap (2–3 s). Let it cool.
  4. Wrap each joint separately with tape, then tape all four cable wires together, and the jacket ends over them.
  5. Twist the green and white wires loosely together (about one turn per 2 cm).
  6. Fix the bundle with a cable tie on each side of the opening so no pull reaches the joints.
Check. No bare copper visible. Green wire end beeps with contact 3 of the A plug, white with 2, black with 4. None of the three beeps with contact 1 (red, 5 V).

4. Solder the wires to the Pico

4. Solder the wires to the Pico (continued)

  1. Lay the Pico component side up, micro-USB at the top. Count pins down the left edge from the top: 1, 2, 3 … Find pin 13. Many Picos have the pin names printed on the underside: if yours does, confirm GND, GP10 and GP11 there.
  2. Shorten the three wires to the length you need (under 10 cm from the cable). Strip 2 mm and tin.
  3. Black (GND) into pin 13, from the underside; solder on the component side.
  4. Green (D+) into pin 14.
  5. White (D-) into pin 15.
  6. Trim the ends flush.
Check. Pin 13 beeps with contact 4 of the A plug (GND), pin 14 with contact 3 (D+), pin 15 with contact 2 (D-). No beep between pins 13, 14, 15 and 16, or between any of them and contact 1. The red wire is not connected to the Pico.

5. Test and use

  1. Plug the extension into a computer and a USB mouse into its socket. The mouse must work as before. If not, a splice is broken or bridged: recheck section 2.
  2. Flash the firmware (separate guide) if not done yet.
  3. At the instrument: close the vendor software, unplug the instrument's USB cable from the PC, plug the extension into the PC and the instrument's cable into the extension.
  4. Connect the Pico to the laptop with the micro-USB cable. LibreTap's Connections shows USB sniffer ready (Pico on …, USB Sniffer Stream).
  5. Low-speed device (1.5 Mbit/s: keyboards, some old devices)? Tick that in LibreTap ▸ Advanced…. Instruments are almost always full speed: leave it unticked.
  6. Start the vendor software; it must work as before. Press Record in LibreTap.
